What is Forceteller good at?

Forceteller is the app that made saju feel modern in Korea, and its reach shows it: the company reports around nine million users. It covers saju, tarot, Western astrology and dream readings in one place, and the writing is better than the category average.

If you want range, and you want the reading handed to you rather than the chart handed to you, Forceteller is the strongest of the Korean apps.

What is Jeomsin good at?

Jeomsin is the free saju app most Korean readers have tried at least once, and the daily fortune is the part it does best. It also carries a manseryeok, the perpetual calendar tool that Korean practitioners actually use to look a chart up.

If you want a daily reading in Korean at no cost, this is the default, and it has been for years.

What is Hellobot good at?

Hellobot took the part of fortune telling that is supposed to be fun and built the whole product around it. Answers arrive as short messages from characters you pick, which is a much gentler way in than a wall of stems and branches.

It has been one of the most downloaded fortune apps in Korea, and the reason is design rather than depth. If saju has ever felt intimidating, start here.

What are the Japanese 四柱推命 sites good at?

Japan has both: well-established 四柱推命 websites and a real set of apps. The free 命式 tools on sites like unkoi.com are genuinely good. You get a chart in a browser with nothing to install, in Japanese, using Japanese vocabulary.

The detailed write-ups are usually sold one at a time, which suits someone who wants one careful reading rather than an app they open every day.

What are the Chinese fortune portals good at?

Sites like 科技紫微網 have been publishing Chinese fortune content since the early web, and the catalogue is enormous. For 紫微斗數 in particular, which Gilsung does not do at all, they are the deep end.

If you read Chinese and you want a long, specific report written by people who have been at this for decades, this is where that lives.

What are the free web calculators good at?

Master Sean Chan's calculator and MingDecode both give you a clean BaZi chart in a browser for nothing, in English. English-language BaZi is thin on the ground, and these two carry a lot of it.

They are also the best second opinion you can get. If two apps disagree about your chart, a third source settles which rule each one is using.

Why does my chart differ between two of these apps?

Because they use different calendar rules, not because one is broken. Four rules cause nearly all of it. Does the year begin at lichun or at Lunar New Year? Do months follow the solar terms? Does the hour use true local solar time, and does a birth after 23:00 roll the day?

Which pillar disagrees tells you which rule they disagree about. We wrote a separate explainer with a test case you can run on any calculator, including the free ones above.

Why is birth data a special case?

You can change a password. You cannot change where and when you were born. Those three details stay true about you for life, and together they point at one person quite precisely.

A Four Pillars app also holds more than your own details. Compatibility means typing in someone else's birth date and time, usually a partner or a family member who never agreed to anything. That is a second person's permanent data sitting in an app they have never opened.

If a company never received your data, they cannot sell it. Nobody can buy it. Not even by buying the whole company.

How do you check any BaZi app's privacy yourself?

Open the app's page in the store and read the privacy section, which is what the developer told Apple or Google about their own app. It takes about thirty seconds and it is better evidence than any marketing page, including this one.

  • Find App Privacy on the App Store, or Data safety on Google Play.
  • Read Data Used to Track You and Data Linked to You. Those two matter most.
  • An app that collects nothing shows one line: Data Not Collected. Gilsung's says that.
  • Check whether it needs a sign-in. A sign-in means a copy of your details on someone else's computer.
  • Turn the internet off and open it. An app that still draws your chart is doing the work on your phone.

The one thing the labels will not tell you

There is no label category for "this app sends your chart to an outside service to be written up." A privacy label can look modest whether the app does that or not. The label is the first check, not the last one.

Read the privacy policy and look for named outside companies, then run the internet-off test. In Chinese app stores some fortune apps have started advertising that they collect nothing. That is good for everybody. The label is still where you go to confirm it.

Gilsung is written in English, Japanese, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Thai and Indonesian as well as Korean. The free English web calculators are the other option and they cost nothing. The large Korean apps are excellent and are written for Korean readers.

Do I have to talk to a fortune teller to get a saju reading?

No. Several popular apps are built around paid readings from a person, which a lot of people specifically want. Gilsung is the other kind: it computes and reads your chart itself, with no fortune tellers and nothing to recharge.

Which BaZi app works offline?

Gilsung works with the internet turned off, because every calculation and every reading happens on your device. The internet-off test is the quickest way to judge any app here. An app that cannot draw your chart on a plane is having it drawn somewhere else.

Are there BaZi apps without a subscription?

Gilsung has no subscription. Your four pillars, your day master reading and your daily reading are free, and the extras are one-time purchases. Most of the large fortune apps are free to start and then charge per report, which is a different model rather than a worse one.
